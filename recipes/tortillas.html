Tortillas

Although corn is used to prepare a wide variety of foods, one of them—the tortilla—stands out for a simple reason: it is eaten every day. This is because it possesses qualities that make it the ideal accompaniment to virtually any dish. Tortillas can be eaten in their original form or transformed into part of other dishes, such as chilaquiles or enchiladas. They provide the perfect vessel for practically any ingredient and can be turned into tacos or quesadillas. Although there is a standard size for tortillas, they can be found in different dimensions depending on their intended use.

In the past, all tortillas were prepared by hand, whenever possible at the very moment they were going to be eaten, because one condition these marvelous discs of corn demand is that they be served hot. Eating cold tortillas is synonymous with neglect, hardship, and barely getting by in life. According to Salvador Novo, once the tortilla was placed on the comal, “…it would puff up as if it had come to life, as if it wanted to fly, to rise; as if Ehécatl had breathed life into it. It was the moment to gently remove it from the comalli; when it already had upon the flesh of our flesh, our sustenance, another epidermis”

Like other practices associated with corn, tortilla-making has undergone transformations after remaining essentially unchanged for millennia. Today, the vast majority of tortillas that reach our tables are produced in what are known as tortillerías, where they are mechanically made using machines of Mexican ingenuity—it could hardly have been otherwise—or they are manufactured industrially and sold in plastic bags.

The tradition of making tortillas by hand is now largely limited to rural areas and certain restaurants that offer them as a kind of luxury, which they certainly are. Fortunately, in markets it is still possible to find women who make tortillas by hand and sell them by the dozen, using methods remarkably similar to those followed by our ancestors for thousands of years.
